How the number is built. Hourly telemetry becomes daily component health signals in the database (2,301,600 rows over 548 days). A gradient-boosted hazard model per component predicts failure inside 21 days, trained on the earlier period and scored on the later one; its probabilities are calibrated and a component whose model cannot beat its own base rate falls back to that base rate and says so. Those probabilities multiply an itemised failure cost — repair, recovery, driver hours, contract penalty, lost revenue — to give the expected cost of leaving each component in service. A mixed-integer program then assigns jobs to bay-days to minimise total expected cost, and HiGHS proves the solution optimal rather than merely reporting the best it found.

What is real and what is not. The engine is real and runs on your data unchanged. The fleet is simulated: src/bayline/simulate/ generates it from a seed, and it is the only module a customer replaces. Prices come from config/bayline.yaml and are hashed into every plan, so any schedule can be traced to the assumptions that produced it.
